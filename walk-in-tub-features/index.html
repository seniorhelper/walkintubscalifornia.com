Features in California homes

Choosing walk-in tub features for a California home

Feature lists can be long. The safety basics matter for everyone; the comfort features matter only if they will get the person into the tub more often. Here is what each one does.

Two walk-in tubs can look alike in a photo and feel completely different in use. The door, the seat, the controls and the drain decide whether the tub is easy and safe day after day. The jets and heaters decide how enjoyable it is. It helps to separate the two groups before you look at prices.

Safety basics every tub should have

Low threshold

The step over the door sill is the reason to buy a walk-in tub. Ask for the exact step-in height of each model and compare it with what the person can manage comfortably.

A seat at a comfortable height

The built-in seat should let the person sit with feet flat on the tub floor and stand up without straining. A contoured seat helps people who slide forward. Ask about seat height and seat depth.

Grab bars inside and out

Look for a bar near the door for getting in, and a bar near the seat for standing up. The National Institute on Aging recommends grab bars both inside and outside the tub. Plan a wall-mounted bar outside the tub as part of the installation.

Textured floor

A slip-resistant tub floor reduces the chance of feet sliding when standing up or stepping out.

Controls within reach of the seat

The person should be able to start the fill, adjust temperature, turn jets on and open the drain without leaning forward. Large buttons or lever handles are easier for hands with arthritis.

Temperature control

Older skin can burn quickly, and some health conditions reduce the ability to feel heat. The California Plumbing Code limits the hot water delivered by a bathtub or whirlpool tub filler to 120 degrees Fahrenheit, using a temperature-limiting device. Make sure the installer sets and confirms it.

Either way, the door cannot be opened until the water has drained. That is by design: the weight of the water would flood the floor. Plan for the drain time as part of the bath.

Comfort and therapy features

Water jets (whirlpool or hydrotherapy)

A pump circulates bath water through adjustable nozzles aimed at the back, hips, legs or feet. Many people with sore muscles or stiff joints enjoy the targeted massage. Jets need regular cleaning per the manufacturer's instructions, because water stays in the lines.

Air jets

A blower sends warm air through many small holes in the tub floor and seat, making a gentle, effervescent feel. Many air systems run a purge cycle after the bath to blow out remaining water, so less water sits in the lines.

Heated seat and backrest

Because you sit in an empty tub while it fills and in a draining tub at the end, a heated surface makes those minutes more comfortable, especially in a cooler bathroom.

In-line heater

Keeps the bath water warm while jets run. It does not heat cold water from scratch; your water heater still has to supply the hot water for the fill.

Fast drain

Pump-assisted or larger drains shorten the wait at the end of the bath. Real-world drain time also depends on the size and condition of your home's drain line, so ask what to expect in your house.

Hand-held shower

Useful for rinsing hair and for caregivers who help with bathing. In California, showerheads, including hand-held showerheads, sold for homes must meet the state's appliance efficiency standards, which limit flow to 1.8 gallons per minute.

Other extras

Colored lighting (chromotherapy), aromatherapy, neck pillows and foot massage features are mainly about enjoyment. They are fine to have if they make bathing something the person looks forward to, which increases how often the tub gets used.

California details that shape feature choices

The same feature list reads differently depending on where the tub is going. A few California conditions come up again and again:

  • Hard water in much of Southern California. The City of San Diego, for example, reports average hardness of about 16 grains per gallon. Minerals build up in jet lines and on drain parts, so in hard-water areas a simpler jet system, or a soaking-only model, can mean less upkeep. Check your water supplier's annual water quality report.
  • Cool coastal bathrooms. In San Francisco, the Peninsula and much of the coast, unheated older bathrooms stay cool year-round. That is where a heated seat or backrest earns its place.
  • Older electrical panels. Many pre-1970 California homes have small or crowded panels. Powered features need a dedicated circuit, so a panel photo early in the process avoids surprises.
  • Water heater size and earthquake strapping. A deep fill can outrun a small tank. If you upgrade the heater, state law requires it to be braced or strapped against earthquakes. See our older California homes guide.
  • Desert and valley heat. In Palm Springs, Fresno or the Inland Empire, an in-line heater matters less in summer than a comfortable, moderate water temperature and a cool room.

How to decide what you need

  1. Pick the safety basics first. Threshold height, seat, grab bars, controls and temperature limit. These are non-negotiable.
  2. Choose the door from the person's mobility and the floor space you measured.
  3. Ask the person what would make them look forward to a bath. Warmth? Massage? A shorter wait? Add only those features.
  4. Check the house. Electrical capacity and water heater size can rule features in or out.

Warranty questions to ask: What does the manufacturer cover on the shell, the door seal, the pumps and the heater, and for how long? Who covers installation workmanship? Get the answers in writing before you buy.

Questions people ask

Are air jets or water jets better?

They feel different. Water jets (whirlpool) push water through nozzles for a firmer massage. Air jets blow warm air bubbles for a gentler, all-over feel. Some tubs have both. If the person has sensitive skin or just wants to relax, air jets are often enough.

Does a walk-in tub need a fast drain?

It is not required, but it is one of the most requested features, because the person waits seated in the tub while it drains. A faster drain shortens that wait. Actual drain time also depends on your home's drain line.

What is an anti-scald valve?

A valve that limits the hottest water reaching the tub, to reduce the risk of burns. The California Plumbing Code caps the hot water temperature delivered by a bathtub filler at 120 degrees Fahrenheit. Older adults with reduced sensation are at higher risk of scalds, so this matters.

Is a heated seat worth it?

For people who get cold easily, yes. You sit in the tub while it fills and drains, and a heated seat or backrest keeps you comfortable during those minutes. If cold is not an issue, it is optional.
