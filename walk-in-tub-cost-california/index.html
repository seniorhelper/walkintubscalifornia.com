Cost guide

What a walk-in tub costs in California, and why quotes vary so much

There is no single price for a walk-in tub. Below are the national ranges that independent publishers report, the parts of a California job that push a number up or down, and a simple way to compare quotes line by line.

Most families who call about a walk-in tub ask the same first question: how much? It is a fair question, and the honest answer is a range plus a list of reasons. A walk-in tub is not a single product with a single price. It is a fixture, a set of options, and an installation job that has to fit a particular bathroom. The same tub can be a simple swap in one house and a bigger project in the house next door.

What independent publishers report nationally

We do not publish a price list on this site, because a number without a site visit or a detailed conversation would only be a guess. What we can do is point you to the ranges that consumer publishers report for the United States as a whole. They are useful for setting expectations, as long as you treat them as broad national figures rather than a California quote.

Published national walk-in tub cost ranges
PublisherWhat they report
NCOA Adviser (2025)About $3,000 to $20,000 for most jobs when tub and installation are added together; installation alone can add $1,000 to $10,000 depending on remodeling.
HomeAdvisor (2025 data)A reported average around $13,100, with a typical range of about $4,000 to $22,100.
This Old House (2025)Tub alone roughly $2,000 to $5,000; installation roughly $2,000 to $10,000 more.

Notice how far apart the low and high ends are. That spread is real. It reflects differences in tub size, materials, jets and heaters, plus the amount of carpentry, tile, plumbing and electrical work involved. Labor and permit costs in many California cities also tend to run higher than in lower-cost regions, so it is sensible to expect a California quote to sit somewhere in the middle or upper part of a national range rather than at the bottom. Your quote depends on your home, not on an average.

The parts of a California job that move the number

1. The tub itself

Size, shape, door direction and features set the base. A compact soaking model costs less than a wide bariatric tub with a heated seat and two jet systems. Material and build quality matter too. Ask what the shell is made of, what the door seal is, and what the manufacturer's warranty actually covers, in writing.

2. Removing what is there now

Pulling out a steel or fiberglass tub is usually straightforward. A cast-iron tub in a 1920s Los Angeles bungalow or a 1940s Bay Area flat can weigh several hundred pounds and may need to be broken up on site. Tile walls that come down with the old tub add demolition and disposal time.

3. Walls around the tub

Many installs use a wall surround kit that covers the area above the new tub. Others need tile repair or new tile. Hidden water damage behind old tile is common in houses that have had the same bathroom for decades, and repairing rotted framing or subfloor adds cost that nobody can see until the old tub is out.

4. Plumbing

A walk-in tub that lands where the old tub sat, with the drain in the same spot, keeps plumbing costs down. Moving the drain, switching from a left-hand to a right-hand drain, or upgrading old galvanized supply lines adds work. On a slab foundation, moving a drain can mean cutting concrete. On a raised foundation, the plumber works from the crawlspace, which is often simpler.

5. Electrical

Jets, in-line heaters and heated seats usually need a dedicated, GFCI-protected circuit. If the electrical panel is full, older, or far from the bathroom, the electrician's part of the job grows. A soaking-only tub with no motors may need no new circuit at all.

6. Water heater capacity

Walk-in tubs are deep, and you fill them while seated, so slow fill times and lukewarm water are a common complaint when the heater is small. Some homeowners add a larger tank or a different heater. In California, any new or replacement tank-type water heater must be strapped or braced against earthquake motion under state law, which is part of the cost if you replace one. See our guide to older California homes for details.

7. Permits and inspections

Permit rules are set by your city or county building department. A like-for-like swap may need little; new circuits, moved plumbing or structural changes generally need a permit. Permit fees are set locally. Ask each contractor whether the permit is included and who will be at the house for inspection.

8. Access and floors

Second-floor bathrooms, narrow hallways, tight stairwells in San Francisco row houses, or a condo building with elevator reservations all add time. A filled walk-in tub is heavy, so on an upper floor the installer should look at the floor framing before work begins.

How to compare two quotes fairly

When quotes differ by thousands of dollars, the difference is usually scope, not profit. Put each quote side by side and check that each one answers the same questions:

  • Which exact tub model, size and door direction is included?
  • Which features are included: air jets, water jets, heated seat, in-line heater, fast drain, hand shower?
  • Is removal and disposal of the old tub included?
  • What happens to the walls: surround kit, tile repair, new tile?
  • Is a dedicated electrical circuit included if the tub needs one?
  • Who pulls the permit, and is the permit fee in the price?
  • What happens, and what does it cost, if rotted subfloor or framing turns up?
  • What warranties apply to the tub, and which apply to the installation labor?
  • What is the payment schedule, and is the down payment within California limits?

That last point matters. Under California law, a home improvement down payment generally cannot be more than $1,000 or 10 percent of the contract price, whichever is less. The Contractors State License Board (CSLB) explains this and other contract rules in its guide for seniors. Our licensed contractor guide walks through the rest.

Ways families keep the project within budget

  • Keep the drain where it is. Choosing a tub whose drain side and footprint match the old tub is the single biggest lever on labor.
  • Buy the features you will use. A heated seat or jets make sense if they will get you into the tub more often. If not, a soaking model gives the same safer entry for less.
  • Ask about delivery only. If you already work with a licensed contractor you trust, ask whether the tub can be shipped for your contractor to install.
  • Check funding before you sign. Veterans, Medi-Cal members and some lower-income older adults may have options. Read paying for a walk-in tub first, because some programs must approve work before it starts.
  • Consider a shower instead. If the person mostly showers, a low-threshold walk-in shower may solve the problem. Our tub versus shower guide compares them.

Red flags on price

Be careful with any seller who offers a price that is only good if you sign today, asks for a large deposit up front, or will not leave a written contract for you to review with family. A reputable company will give you time. California also gives buyers aged 65 and older a longer right to cancel a home improvement contract than other buyers; the CSLB senior guide explains the current rule. If you feel rushed, it is fine to say you need a day to think.

Questions people ask

Is there a typical price for a walk-in tub in California?

No single figure is reliable. National publishers report wide ranges, for example roughly $3,000 to $20,000 for most jobs according to NCOA's 2025 guide. Your quote depends on the tub model, the features you pick, and how much plumbing, electrical and wall work your bathroom needs.

Why did two companies give me very different numbers?

Quotes often cover different scopes. One may include removal of the old tub, wall surround, permit and a dedicated electrical circuit, while another lists only the tub. Ask each company for an itemized scope and compare the same items side by side.

Can I lower the cost by using my own contractor?

Sometimes. Aging Safely Baths & More can ship a tub without installation so your own licensed contractor can install it. Whether that saves money depends on your contractor's labor rate and on what your bathroom needs.

Does a walk-in tub raise my water heater costs?

It can. Walk-in tubs hold more water than many standard tubs, and you fill them while seated, so a small or older water heater may not keep up. Ask the installer to check your heater's capacity before you choose a model.
