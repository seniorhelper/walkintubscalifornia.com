Medi-Cal home modifications for a safer bathroom in California

Medi-Cal can pay for some bathroom changes, but not through the front door most people try. This guide explains the CalAIM home modification benefit, checks your county's Medi-Cal plans against the state's own list, and ranks the other California routes worth a phone call.

By the Aging Safely Baths editorial team. Updated October 22, 2026.

Short answer: Medi-Cal has no walk-in tub benefit, but many Medi-Cal managed care plans offer a CalAIM Community Support called Environmental Accessibility Adaptations. It pays up to a $7,500 lifetime maximum for medically needed home changes, such as a roll-in shower, for members at risk of nursing-home care. Ask your plan before any work starts.

Families usually reach this question after a fall, a hospital stay, or a therapist saying the tub has become unsafe. The good news is that California has more public help for bathroom safety than most states. The hard part is that the help is split across a health plan benefit, several Medi-Cal waivers, the aging network, the VA and the tax code, each with its own rules and its own front door. This page sorts them out and links every claim to the agency that makes the rule.

What is the Medi-Cal home modification benefit?

Since 2022, California's Medi-Cal managed care plans have been able to offer a menu of services the state calls Community Supports, part of the CalAIM reforms. One of them is Environmental Accessibility Adaptations, usually shortened to EAA or simply called home modifications. In its Community Supports Policy Guide (Volume 1, updated April 2025), the Department of Health Care Services (DHCS) defines these as physical changes to a home that are needed for the member's health, welfare and safety, or to let the member live more independently, where the member would otherwise need institutional care.

DHCS gives a short list of examples. The ones that matter in a bathroom are grab bars, doorway widening for a wheelchair user, and making a bathroom and shower wheelchair accessible, with a roll-in shower named as the example. The same list includes stair lifts, ramps, specialized electrical or plumbing work needed for medical equipment, and a personal emergency response system for people who are alone much of the day.

Community Supports are optional for plans. DHCS says plans are strongly encouraged to offer them, but each plan decides which ones it covers in each county. That is why the checker below matters: it uses the state's own county-by-plan table instead of guessing.

Does Medi-Cal pay for a walk-in tub?

Not as a standard benefit, and not by name. Walk-in tubs do not appear in the DHCS examples, while roll-in showers do. That does not mean a walk-in tub is ruled out. It means the plan will look hard at whether the specific change is medically necessary for the specific person, whether something simpler would do the same job, and whether the cost fits within the benefit. Three rules in the policy guide shape that decision:

  • Cheaper covered items come first. If a regular Medi-Cal benefit such as durable medical equipment (a shower chair or transfer bench, for example) would meet the same goal, DHCS says that service should be used instead.
  • The $7,500 lifetime maximum. The only exceptions DHCS lists are a move to a new residence, or a change in the member's condition so significant that more modifications are needed.
  • Direct medical benefit only. Changes of general use to the household, and aesthetic upgrades, are excluded. Finishing work such as drywall and paint is allowed to return the room to a usable state.

In practice, a tub-to-shower conversion with grab bars is the project these rules were written for. If the person truly needs to soak, for example for a condition a doctor documents, ask the plan whether it would apply the benefit toward a walk-in tub and what documentation it wants. Our California cost guide explains why a full walk-in tub project often costs more than $7,500, so families sometimes combine sources or choose a walk-in shower instead.

Which Medi-Cal plans offer home modifications in my county?

DHCS publishes a table of which Community Supports each Medi-Cal managed care plan has chosen to offer in each county. In the version updated June 2026, we counted 115 plan-and-county rows. Of those, 88 list Environmental Accessibility Adaptations, 25 do not, and 2 are unclear in the published file. Most of the large Southern California and Bay Area plans list it. The biggest gap we found is in the counties served by Partnership Health Plan of California, where the table does not list home modifications.

How do I ask my Medi-Cal plan for home modifications?

The policy guide spells out what a plan must collect before it can approve the work. Knowing the list ahead of time saves weeks.

  1. Call member services and use the right words. Ask whether your plan offers the "Environmental Accessibility Adaptations (Home Modifications)" Community Support, and how to be assessed for it. If the person already has an Enhanced Care Management care manager, start there.
  2. Get a doctor's order. The plan must have an order from the member's primary care physician or another health professional that names the equipment or change being requested.
  3. Expect a therapy evaluation. Unless the plan waives it, a physical or occupational therapist evaluates the person, explains why the current setup does not work, and explains how the requested change lowers the risk of institutional care. DHCS says this should usually come from someone with no tie to the contractor.
  4. Collect bids. If possible, the plan wants at least two itemized bids covering the work, cost, labor and warranties. Physical changes must be done by a California-licensed contractor; use our CSLB license check guide.
  5. Allow a home visit. The plan must confirm through a home visit that the change suits the home. Have measurements ready; our measuring guide shows which ones.
  6. Get owner consent if you rent. Written permission from the owner is required for anything installed in a home the member does not own.
  7. Wait for written approval. DHCS gives plans 90 days from the request to assess and authorize, unless more time is needed for owner consent or the member asks for longer. Before work starts, the plan must tell the owner and member in writing that the changes are permanent and that the state will not maintain or remove them.

The HCBA Waiver

The Home and Community-Based Alternatives Waiver serves Medi-Cal members of any age whose needs match a nursing-facility level of care but who want to stay at home. Environmental accessibility adaptations are one of its services, alongside care management, personal care and respite. Enrollment runs through waiver agencies, and DHCS has kept a waitlist since July 2023 with priority for people moving out of facilities. A person cannot be enrolled in two of these waivers at the same time, so ask the waiver agency how its adaptations benefit fits with anything your health plan already approved.

California Community Transitions

CCT helps Medi-Cal members who have been in a nursing facility or hospital for a long stay move back to a home of their own. DHCS describes the threshold as 60 consecutive days in a licensed health facility. Disability Rights California notes the program can pay for home modifications such as grab bars and ramps. If a parent is in a skilled nursing facility now and the bathroom is what stands between them and coming home, ask the facility social worker about CCT this week.

MSSP and IHSS

The Multipurpose Senior Services Program, overseen by the California Department of Aging, offers care management to older Medi-Cal members who are certified or certifiable for nursing-facility placement, and CDA lists minor home repair among its services. In-Home Supportive Services, run by counties under the California Department of Social Services, pays for in-home help based on a social worker's assessment of what the person cannot safely do alone. Neither one is a remodeling fund, but both put a professional in the home who can document the bathroom risk for the plan.

Your Area Agency on Aging

California's Area Agencies on Aging run fall prevention programs with state funding. CDA's Dignity at Home Fall Prevention Program covers people 60 and older or with a disability, with household income at or below 80 percent of area median income, who have fallen or are at risk of falling or of institutional care. It can pay for fall prevention equipment, materials and labor. That usually means grab bars, hand-held showers and transfer benches rather than a full tub, but those items alone remove much of the risk. See our California aging resources guide for regional agencies.

Medicare and Medicare Advantage

Original Medicare generally does not pay for walk-in tubs or bathroom remodels. Some Medicare Advantage plans include extra home safety benefits, which vary by plan and year; the national picture is covered in this Medicare Advantage bathroom safety guide from Aging Safely Baths. For free, unbiased help comparing plans, call California's HICAP counselors at 1-800-434-0222.

Veterans: HISA and CalVet

The VA's HISA benefit pays up to $6,800 over a lifetime for a service-connected condition, or for any condition if the veteran is rated 50 percent or more service-connected, and up to $2,000 for others. VA excludes spa, hot tub and whirlpool-type tubs, so a roll-in or walk-in shower is usually the cleaner HISA project; see the HISA bathroom guide for the national details. Veterans who already have a CalVet Home Loan can also ask CalVet about its home improvement loans, which are loans rather than grants. A County Veterans Service Office will help with the paperwork at no charge.

The California property tax exclusion

Many families do not know this one. Under Revenue and Taxation Code section 74.3, construction that makes an existing home more accessible for a severely and permanently disabled person who lives there can be excluded from reassessment, so the improvement does not raise the property tax bill. The home must be owner-occupied and eligible for the homeowners' exemption, the resident with the disability does not have to be the owner, and a licensed physician signs the claim, form BOE-63. The exclusion does not cover features that are standard in comparable homes. Ask your county assessor for the current form and filing timing before the project starts.

Questions people ask

Will Medi-Cal pay for a walk-in tub in California?

Not as a standard benefit. Many Medi-Cal managed care plans offer home modifications through CalAIM Community Supports, up to a $7,500 lifetime maximum, for members at risk of nursing-home care. DHCS names a roll-in shower as an example, not a walk-in tub, so the plan decides case by case. Ask before work starts.

How much can Medi-Cal pay for home modifications?

The Community Supports home modification benefit has a total lifetime maximum of $7,500. DHCS allows exceptions only if the member moves to a new home or the member's condition changes so much that more modifications are needed to stay safely at home.

Does my Medi-Cal plan offer home modifications?

DHCS publishes a county-by-plan table. In the June 2026 version, 88 of 115 plan-and-county rows list home modifications. Use the checker on this page to see your county, then confirm with member services, because plans can change what they offer.

What paperwork does the plan need?

An order from the primary care physician or another health professional, usually a physical or occupational therapy evaluation, if possible two itemized bids from licensed contractors, a home visit, and written owner consent if the member rents.

How long does approval take?

DHCS gives plans 90 days from the request to assess and authorize home modifications, unless more time is needed to get homeowner consent or the member asks for a longer time frame.

Is there a California grant for walk-in tubs for seniors?

There is no statewide walk-in tub grant. The closest options are Medi-Cal Community Supports, the HCBA Waiver, California Community Transitions, Area Agency on Aging fall prevention programs for smaller items, and the VA HISA benefit for veterans.

Will a bathroom accessibility remodel raise my property taxes?

It may not have to. California lets owner-occupied homes exclude construction that makes the home more accessible for a severely and permanently disabled resident from reassessment. A physician signs form BOE-63, which you file with your county assessor.
