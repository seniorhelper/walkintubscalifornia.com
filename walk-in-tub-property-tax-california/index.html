Walk-in tub and property taxes in California: keep it off the bill

One form, BOE-63, can exclude accessibility work from reassessment.

Get a free quoteCall 1-888-779-2284

Short answer: A walk-in tub usually does not raise California property taxes, and when it might, the law gives you a way out. Revenue and Taxation Code section 74.3 excludes accessibility work done for a severely and permanently disabled resident from reassessment. You claim it on form BOE-63 with a physician's statement. Without it, an assessed improvement is taxed at about 1% a year.

Families pricing a walk-in tub for a parent in California tend to ask about Medicare, Medi-Cal and the VA. Property tax comes up later, usually when someone remembers that Proposition 13 rewards owners who have held a home for decades, and worries that a remodel will undo it. It will not undo the whole thing, and for accessibility work it may not touch the bill at all. Here is how the assessor looks at a bathroom project, and how to use the disabled access exclusion if you need it.

When does a bathroom project get reassessed in California?

Under Proposition 13, the general property tax is capped at 1% of assessed value, plus any voter-approved taxes, and a home's assessed value can rise no more than 2% a year. A change in ownership resets the value. So does "new construction," but only for the new part: the State Board of Equalization explains that completed new construction gets its own base year value equal to the market value it adds, while the rest of the home keeps its old base.

Not every project counts as new construction. The Board says normal maintenance and repairs are typically not assessed, and gives replacing a shake roof with a tile roof as an example of work that is not new construction. On the other side of the line, a kitchen or bathroom renovation that changes the structure or the floor plan can be assessed. Borderline jobs are decided by the county assessor, case by case.

What does the section 74.3 disabled access exclusion cover?

Section 74.3 says that new construction does not include work done to make an existing home more accessible to a severely and permanently disabled person who lives there permanently. It applies to single-family and multiple-family dwellings that qualify for the homeowners' exemption, and to work completed on or after June 6, 1990.

  • Who must qualify. A permanent resident with a physical disability or impairment, from birth, accident or disease, that limits employment or substantially limits one or more major life activities, diagnosed as permanently affecting their ability to function. The statute names disabilities that affect sight, speech, hearing or the use of any limbs.
  • Who must own. Anyone. The Board of Equalization notes that you do not have to own the home to use the exclusion, but the home has to be owner-occupied and eligible for the homeowners' exemption. An adult child who owns the home a parent lives in is a common case.
  • What is excluded. Only the features that adapt the home for that person. Other upgrades done at the same time are assessed normally unless they are merely incidental.
  • What is not covered. An entirely new house.
  • How long it lasts. Until a new base year value is set, which usually means a change in ownership.

There is a second, similar-sounding exclusion in section 74.6, claimed on form BOE-63-A. That one is for accessibility work on other kinds of property under the Americans with Disabilities Act, and it specifically does not cover owner-occupied homes that qualify under 74.3. For a walk-in tub in a family home, BOE-63 is the form you want.

How do you file a BOE-63 claim?

  1. Call your county assessor before the work starts. Ask whether the project is likely to be treated as new construction at all. If the answer is "probably not," you may not need the claim, but it costs little to file.
  2. Get the physician's statement. The claim needs a statement signed by a licensed physician or surgeon of an appropriate specialty certifying that the person is severely and permanently disabled and identifying the disability-related needs the work addresses.
  3. Get a statement of the work. List the specific construction or modifications that were necessary. An itemized contractor quote that separates the low step-in threshold, door, seat, grab bars and anti-scald valve from any cosmetic work does most of this for you.
  4. File BOE-63 with the county assessor. The disabled person, a spouse or a legal guardian signs. Forms are on each county assessor's website; the assessor may charge a processing fee.
  5. Keep copies. If the property later changes hands, the paperwork shows what was excluded and why.

The Board of Equalization lists no set filing period for this exclusion, but filing before or soon after completion avoids a supplemental bill you then have to unwind. Questions people ask

Does a walk-in tub raise property taxes in California?

Often it does not. Swapping an old tub for a walk-in tub can be treated as a replacement rather than new construction, and the Board of Equalization says normal maintenance and repairs are typically not assessed. A remodel that changes the room's structure or floor plan can be assessed, but if the work is for a severely and permanently disabled resident, Revenue and Taxation Code section 74.3 lets you exclude it.

What is form BOE-63?

BOE-63 is the Disabled Persons Claim for Exclusion of New Construction Improvements. You file it with your county assessor along with a statement from a licensed physician or surgeon certifying the disability and a statement identifying the accessibility work that was done.

Who counts as severely and permanently disabled for this exclusion?

Section 74.3 covers a person with a physical disability or impairment, from birth, accident or disease, that limits employment or substantially limits one or more major life activities and is diagnosed as permanent. The statute names disabilities that affect sight, speech, hearing or the use of any limbs.

Do I have to own the home to use the exclusion?

No. The disabled person must live in the home as a permanent resident, but the owner can be someone else, such as an adult child. The home must be owner-occupied and qualify for the homeowners' exemption.

Is there a deadline to file the BOE-63 claim?

The Board of Equalization lists no stated filing period for the section 74.3 exclusion. Filing before or right after the work is finished keeps things simple, and your county assessor can tell you how it handles late claims.

What happens to the exclusion when the house is sold?

It lasts until a new base year value is set, which usually happens at a change in ownership. At that point the excluded improvement becomes part of the property's new assessed value like everything else.
