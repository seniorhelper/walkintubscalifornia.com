Walk-in tubs in San Francisco and the Bay Area

In much of the Bay Area, getting into the house is half the challenge: a flight of stairs from the sidewalk to living space above a garage, or a narrow staircase in a Victorian flat. Plan the route as carefully as the tub.

Bay Area homes are some of the hardest in California to adapt for aging in place, and some of the most worth adapting, because people tend to stay in them for decades. The common obstacles are stairs, small bathrooms, older plumbing and limited parking. None of them is a reason to give up, but each should be planned for.

Bay Area homes and their bathrooms

Homes over garages

Large parts of San Francisco's Sunset and Richmond districts, Daly City and other Peninsula neighborhoods were built with the living space above a ground-floor garage. The bathroom is on the main living level, up a flight of stairs from the street. That raises two questions: can the tub be carried up the stairs, and can the person manage those stairs for years to come? Some families add a stair lift or plan a ground-floor bathroom in the garage level, where local rules allow. Either way, measure the stairs before choosing a tub.

Victorian and Edwardian flats

Older flats in San Francisco and Oakland have narrow, sometimes steep interior stairs and bathrooms that were added or remodeled long after the house was built. Floors can be out of level, and a walk-in tub must be set level for its door to seal. The installer may need to shim or reinforce the floor. Plumbing may include old cast-iron drains and galvanized supply lines.

East Bay hill homes

In the Oakland and Berkeley hills, many homes sit on sloping lots with long exterior stairways. Delivery access is the main challenge.

Suburban homes

Contra Costa and the southern East Bay have many single-story ranch homes from the 1950s to the 1970s, often with a standard tub in the hall bath. These are usually simpler projects.

Senior communities

Walnut Creek's Rossmoor and other age-restricted communities have their own alteration approval processes. Check with the community's management before you sign a contract.

Cool, foggy bathrooms

Summer fog keeps much of San Francisco and the coast cool year-round, and many older homes have little or no heat in the bathroom. Because you sit in a walk-in tub while it fills and while it drains, those minutes can feel cold. Features that help:

  • A heated seat or backrest
  • An in-line heater to keep the bath warm during a longer soak
  • A fast drain to shorten the wait at the end
  • A licensed electrician-installed bathroom heater or a warm towel within reach

Older Bay Area homes may have limited electrical capacity, so confirm the panel can support a dedicated circuit before choosing powered features.

Parking and logistics

In dense neighborhoods, a delivery truck and an installer's van need somewhere to park. In San Francisco and parts of Oakland and Berkeley, that may mean arranging temporary parking with the city or asking neighbors to keep a space clear. Discuss it before installation day.

Local aging resources

In San Francisco, the Department of Disability and Aging Services (part of the Human Services Agency) runs a Benefits and Resource Hub with a helpline at 415-355-6700 and a walk-in service center at 2 Gough Street. Each other Bay Area county has its own Area Agency on Aging. The statewide line, 1-800-510-2020, routes you to yours. Ask about home safety and fall prevention, caregiver support, and free Medicare counseling.

Earthquakes and water heaters

The Bay Area knows earthquakes well. If you replace a water heater to handle a deep walk-in tub, California law requires it to be strapped or braced. In a small home, ask whether a different heater location or type makes sense, and confirm the strapping is in the quote.

Veterans

The San Francisco VA Health Care System and the VA Palo Alto Health Care System serve Bay Area veterans. Contact the prosthetics service about HISA before buying. VA lists walk-in and roll-in showers as eligible examples and excludes spa, hot tub and Jacuzzi-type tubs.

A Bay Area checklist

  • Measure every stair, turn and landing on the way to the bathroom.
  • Check that the bathroom floor is level, or ask the installer how they will level the tub.
  • Photograph the electrical panel and water heater.
  • Plan parking for installation day.
  • Check the installer's CSLB license, and any HOA or community approvals.

Questions people ask

My San Francisco home has stairs from the street. Can a walk-in tub still be delivered?

Often, yes, but it needs planning. Measure the stair width, any turns, landing sizes and ceiling heights, and send photos. Some models are lighter or narrower than others, and in some homes a walk-in shower is the more practical choice.

Who helps older adults in San Francisco?

The Department of Disability and Aging Services, part of the San Francisco Human Services Agency, runs a Benefits and Resource Hub with a helpline at 415-355-6700 and a walk-in center at 2 Gough Street. The statewide line, 1-800-510-2020, also connects you.

Bathrooms here are cold. What helps?

A heated seat and an in-line heater make the time before and after the bath more comfortable. Make sure any added electrical work is done on a proper circuit, and consider a bathroom heater installed by an electrician.

Do you serve the East Bay and Marin?

Yes. Communities across Alameda, Contra Costa, San Mateo and Marin counties are within the region we help. Call with your ZIP code to confirm installation availability.
